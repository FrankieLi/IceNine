Cost against accuracy, all methods

You asked for the frontier of cost against accuracy for every method tried so far. Each point below is one method run on one sample. Left is cheaper. Down is more accurate. A method is on the frontier when no other method on the same sample is both cheaper and more accurate. A line joins those methods, and each has a ring.

  • Cost is the number of evaluations of the whole run divided by the number of voxels. An evaluation is one call of the cost function for one voxel and one orientation. The axis is logarithmic.
  • Accuracy is the share of voxels that end more than 1 degree from the true orientation, after crystal symmetry. All samples are simulated, so the truth is known.
  • One panel per sample. The methods were run on eight different samples. Two points can be compared only inside one panel.

Terms. BFS is the breadth-first search: a voxel starts its local optimization from a finished neighbour, and a seed is a voxel that needs a global search. Indexed seeds means the seed comes from ray indexing, with the grid search only as a fallback. Stock is the local optimization the search uses by default. CMA is the other local optimization in the code, with a first step of 0.05 degrees; "library CMA" is the same one switched on by the keys the code already has. Paint grains gives each voxel the orientation of a grain already found, with one evaluation. A boundary pass comes after a run: each voxel at a grain boundary gets one short try from each neighbouring grain's orientation.

Grid search. On this page, grid search always means the adaptive grid search that the code runs by default for a voxel with no starting guess. It is not one full grid at a fixed resolution. It starts with a coarse grid over all orientations, keeps the best quarter of the candidates, and then searches a finer grid only around those, level after level. A local optimization of the best candidates ends it. In C++ it is DiscreteAdaptive::ReconstructVoxel, and in Python AdaptiveVoxelReconstructor.reconstruct_voxel, a port of it. No study here used a different grid search. Its cost is not a fixed number: about 48,700 evaluations per voxel on the 200 voxels that are reconstructed alone, and about 332,000 on the pilot's full images. The reason for that difference was not checked again for this page.

What the frontiers say

  • Global search. Ray indexing with a local optimization on the best 2 candidates is on the frontier on the pilot's 69 voxels and on the holdout. On the 200 easy voxels the first candidate alone is already right. On the pilot's 69 voxels the best-2 rule needs about 170 times fewer evaluations than the grid search (1,926 against 332,380), with 0 against 4 wrong. The two intervals overlap, so read that as "not worse".
  • Whole run, grains with gradients. On the first gradient sample, the best trade is run S5 of step 6 with one boundary pass. It is the BFS with indexed seeds, where each seed refines the best 5 of 20 candidates, and the CMA local optimization. It needs 840 evaluations per voxel and leaves 62 wrong (0.25%). The base run needs 4,817 and leaves 203 wrong (0.83%). That is almost 6 times fewer evaluations and fewer wrong voxels. Run S is cheaper still (657) but leaves 100 wrong. Of the 602 voxels that S settles by a try from a neighbour, 59 are wrong before the pass. In step 4's run, with the old seed step, 27 of the same voxels are wrong.
  • Whole run, larger grains (step 7). On the sample with 32 larger grains, S5 is not the cheaper run. With the pass, S5 needs 529 evaluations per voxel and the old seed step (D4) 486, with 30 and 21 wrong. S5 needs no grid search at all there, but it starts 349 seeds, and each seed costs more than an old one. Of those 349, 267 fall in the 6 grains whose gradient is 4 degrees. So which seed step is the better trade depends on the sample. That choice is yours.
  • An early stop in the seed step (step 8). Run S8 is S5 with a seed step that stops at the first refinement whose hit ratio is at least 0.95. With the pass it needs 339 evaluations per voxel on the sample with larger grains (24 wrong) and 733 on the first gradient sample (63 wrong). On both samples that is fewer evaluations than S5 and than D4 with the pass, and the intervals of the wrong counts overlap. The plan's own value (0.80) failed on stored cases, and 0.95 was chosen after that result. Step 9 ran it on a new sample, mid (120 grains, nothing chosen on it): with the pass it costs 26.44 million evaluations and leaves 31 wrong, against 39.09 million and 34 for S5 and 40.56 million and 48 for D4. The intervals overlap. That is one run of each arm on one new sample. It is counted as the confirmation of the stop test (D-20261111-N7); you can overrule that.
  • A start test for the neighbour local optimizations (step 9, run S9). Run S9 is S8 plus one evaluation of the start of each neighbour local optimization: if its hit ratio is at least 0.90, the start is kept and the local optimization is skipped. S9 is cheaper again (22.67 million evaluations on mid, 5.63 million on the sample with larger grains, 14.63 million on the first gradient sample). But it ends with more wrong voxels than S8 on all three samples (40 against 31, 29 against 24, 70 against 63) and a higher median error. So it is on the frontier only as a cheaper, less accurate point. With the pass it breaks more voxels than it fixes against S8 (10 fixed and 15 broken on the larger grains, 31 and 38 on the first gradient sample, 9 and 18 on mid). It is not a candidate for a key (D-20261111-N7).
  • The boundary pass moves a run down. In all twenty runs that have it, the run with the pass has fewer wrong voxels than the same run without it. One pass costs about 125 to 150 evaluations per voxel on the first two samples, about 124 on mid, and about 46 to 48 on the sample with larger grains.
  • Whole run, one orientation per grain. Painting is the cheapest by far, at 54 to 78 evaluations per voxel. This holds only when each grain has a single orientation, which real grains do not.
  • What is left to cut. Run S5 with the pass spends its 840 evaluations per voxel like this: 244 in the seed step, 208 in 15 grid searches where the seed step failed, 238 in the local optimizations and 150 in the boundary pass. A seed and a grid search happen once per grain or piece of a grain. Step 7 repeated the runs on larger grains: there the seed step alone is 57.5% of S5's evaluations. Step 8 cut the seed step (run S8). In S8 with the pass on the larger grains, the largest part is now the local optimizations started from a neighbour: 3.49 of 8.34 million evaluations. Step 9 tested a way to skip those whose start is already good (run S9). It saved evaluations but cost accuracy.
  • No whole-layer run uses a grid search for every seed. On the pilot that run needs 9 times the evaluations of the run with indexed seeds. So the three whole-layer panels start from a baseline that is already reduced.

Limits of these plots

  • One run per point. A difference of a few voxels, such as 57 against 56, is inside the intervals. The table below gives a 95% interval for each share.
  • Evaluations, not time. The cost axis counts calls of the cost function. Pairing the spots (once, 17 s) and the indexing vote (about 0.17 s per voxel) are not evaluations and are not on the axis. Wall time is not shown.
  • Not drawn. Two other keep rules of the boundary pass cost the same as the drawn one and leave more voxels wrong. They are in the table.
  • Left out, because cost and accuracy are not both known for a complete map:
    • the stop rules of AR0 (the page gives only the share of evaluations saved, at most 1.26%);
    • the skip and stop rules of steps 3, 4 and 5 (saved evaluations only);
    • run K of step 5 (stopped by its time cap with no finished map);
    • three BFS variants of AR0 on the pilot (wrong counts, but no evaluation total);
    • splits of a run by voxel group or by gradient level (no evaluations per group).
